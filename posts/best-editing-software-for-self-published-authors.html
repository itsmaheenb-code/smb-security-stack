None of these tools replace a human editor for a final pass — they catch different things, and none of them understand your book's plot or argument. What they're good for is cutting the number of paid editing rounds you need, and catching the mechanical issues before a human editor charges you to find them.

ProWritingAid

ProWritingAid's strength is depth — it generates detailed reports on pacing, repetition, sentence variation, readability, and dozens of other dimensions, and it plugs into Word, Scrivener, and Google Docs so you can edit where you already write. It's the most commonly recommended tool in self-publishing communities for a full manuscript pass before it goes to beta readers or an editor.

Try ProWritingAid

Grammarly

Grammarly is faster and lighter-weight than ProWritingAid — better for catching typos and clarity issues as you type, across your whole browser and desktop, not just inside a manuscript editor. It's less useful for fiction-specific craft feedback, but if you want one tool that also cleans up your emails, blurbs, and social posts, it covers more ground.

Try Grammarly

AutoCrit

AutoCrit is built specifically for fiction: it compares your manuscript against benchmarks from published books in your genre and flags pacing issues, telling-not-showing, dialogue tags, and cliché phrases. Reported pricing varies noticeably by source and promo, so confirm the current rate on their site before subscribing. There's a free tier with unlimited word count but limited tools, which is enough to evaluate the fit.

Where software stops being enough

All three tools operate at the sentence and paragraph level. None of them will tell you your subplot resolves too fast, your protagonist's motivation doesn't track, or your nonfiction argument has a gap in chapter 4. For a book you're charging money for, budget for at least one human editing pass — software gets you a cleaner draft to hand that editor, which usually makes their pass faster and cheaper.
